Who is Jacobo Grinberg
Born in Mexico City in 1946, Grinberg developed a fascination with the human mind from an early age. At just 12 years old, he witnessed his mother die from a brain tumour, and from that point on he decided he would study the human brain. UNILAD Tech He pursued higher education at the National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM), earning a bachelor's degree, then a master's at New York Medical College, and a PhD in psychophysiology from UNAM's Faculty of Sciences, with additional training at New York University's Brain Research Laboratory. Grokipedia He then returned to Mexico and founded his own laboratory at the Universidad Anáhuac. Later, in 1987, Grinberg founded the Instituto Nacional para el Estudio de la Conciencia (National Institute for the Study of Consciousness), where he began to merge his scientific expertise with studies into meditation, shamanism, telepathy, and their connection to brain activity. Core Claims & Theories
─── His Core Claims and Theories His central framework was what he called Syntergic Theory. The theory states that there is a continuous space of energy, and the common human can perceive only a part of it. The result of this process is what everyone understands as reality. This theory tries to answer the question of the creation of experience. Wikipedia The syntergic theory is in close relation with physicist David Bohm's implicate order theory, which conceives space as a holographic sea of infinite potential from which the phenomena we understand as physical things unfold. This enfolded holographic matrix is what Grinberg called the Lattice. The International Space Federation In short, he argued that reality is a neurological projection — the brain interacts with an underlying energy lattice and constructs what we experience as the physical world. He believed focused consciousness could actively interact with that perceptual reality, not just observe it. He also explored the implications of the EPR paradox, which asserts that if quantum theory is a complete model of reality, non-local interactions ought to exist between particles. He set out to prove that a quantum system in the brain creates a non-local, EPR correlation among human brains. Psi Encyclopedia
His Most Solid Research
─── His Most Solid Research His most scientifically credible work was on what he called "transferred potentials" — essentially brain-to-brain correlations. He claimed to have made successful experiments with children who were able to "see" without using their eyes. The subjects were blindfolded, guided in meditation exercises, and then asked to try to perceive printed images by scanning them with their fingers. In some cases, he wrote, the quality of detail and accuracy appeared similar to that of ordinary retinal vision. Psi Encyclopedia His telepathy experiments followed a similar structure — pairs of subjects were isolated from each other, and EEG readings showed correlated brain activity in the receiving subject when the sender was stimulated. An investigation by Grinberg regarding the transfer of potential in the brain was published in Physics Essays, a peer-reviewed scientific journal. The International Space Federation He also published in journals covering neuroscience, psychophysiology, and theoretical biology. In Latin American psi research, Grinberg's establishment of INPEC inspired subsequent laboratories focused on telepathy and extra-sensory perception, with his transferred potential experiments continuing to inform protocols in parapsychological studies. The Long Story · Full Depth
The long story: This is a genuinely fascinating case. Here's everything I can pull together on it. THE MAN Jacobo Grinberg-Zylberbaum was born in Mexico City in [Link] 1946. At just 12 years old, he witnessed his mother die from a brain tumor, and from that point on decided he would dedicate his life to studying the human brain. There's something almost unbearably poetic about that origin point. A child watches consciousness dissolve in front of him, and spends the next 35 years trying to figure out where it actually goes. He graduated from the Psychology School at the National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM), then studied psychophysiology at the Brain Research Institute of New York, where he obtained his PhD. Grinberg founded laboratories for psychological research at UNAM and the University Anáhuac. In 1987 he inaugurated the National Institute for the Study of Consciousness (INPEC), where he carried out most of his investigations. [Link] So this wasn't a fringe character operating out of a garage. Considered by many to be the "Einstein of consciousness," Grinberg took the study of psychophysiology beyond the limits of traditional science in his attempt to map the infinite potential of the human brain. [Link] He had institutional backing, peer-reviewed publications, and a full laboratory. He was inside the system before he started dismantling it. THE THEORY: THE LATTICE This is where it gets weird and worth sitting with seriously. At the foundation of Syntergic Theory lies the concept of the lattice: a pre-physical, informational structure that permeates all of space-time. Perception arises from the congruent interaction between the neural field generated by the brain and the universal energetic structure of the lattice. [Link] Translation: space is not empty. It's a structured information field. And your brain doesn't passively receive the world — it actively generates experience by interacting with that field. What you perceive as "reality" is the output of that collision between your neuronal activity and the underlying fabric of space. The structure of space appears as empty and invisible to our perception; however, each point of space contains the total information of the rest of the points. [Link] Grinberg called this the Lattice. Every coordinate in space contains everything. Every point is the whole. The syntergic theory is in close relation with physicist David Bohm's implicate order theory, which conceives space as a holographic sea of infinite potential from which the phenomena we understand as physical things unfold. This enfolded holographic matrix conforms the "pre-space" or the Lattice, as Grinberg coins it. [Link] This is not pure mysticism. Bohm was one of the most serious physicists of the 20th century. His implicate order was peer-reviewed, wrestled with by serious academics, and never fully refuted. Grinberg was borrowing from hard physics and asking: what does this mean for consciousness specifically? The brain distorts this lattice to create subjective experience, enabling manifestation — the intentional shaping of reality through aligned consciousness. In a "no-self" state, individuals merge with the lattice, transcending ego to manipulate physical outcomes, as observed in Grinberg's studies of shamanic healing. [Link] That last part is where his scientific colleagues stopped returning his calls. But he kept going. THE EXPERIMENT This is the part that's hardest to dismiss. Pairs of subjects were allowed to interact and were then separated inside semisilent Faraday chambers 14.5 meters apart when their EEG activity was registered. Only one subject of each pair was stimulated by 100 flashes. When the stimulated subject showed distinct evoked potentials, the nonstimulated subject showed "transferred potentials" similar to those evoked in the stimulated subject. Control subjects showed no such transferred potentials. [Link] Faraday chambers block all electromagnetic signals. No WiFi, no radio, no conventional transmission of any kind. The two subjects could not see, hear, or otherwise sense each other. One brain was hit with a stimulus. The other brain lit up identically. With no connection between them except the meditation session they'd shared beforehand. He called this phenomenon the "transferred potential," according to which a brain, suitably prepared by a period of meditation, is capable of both proximate and distant interactions with other brains, by a direct involvement of consciousness, and without sensory transmission or local effects. [Link] The paper — "The Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen Paradox in the Brain: The Transferred Potential" — was published in Physics Essays in December 1994. The same month he disappeared. It has since accumulated over 114 citations in academic literature. [Link] This wasn't buried. Other researchers read it, referenced it, and tried to build on it. Many scientists subsequently investigated the topic, assuming that microbiological and psychophysical processes might sustain large-scale and nonlocal information processing inside the human brain. One hypothesis is that microtubules inside neurons might be responsible for quantum computation — the Penrose-Hameroff model. A second additional hypothesis focuses on holographic storage and computation in brain electromagnetic interference patterns — the Holonomic Brain Theory. [Link] In other words, his experiment didn't just sit there gathering dust. It opened a thread that serious physicists and neuroscientists followed. The debate over whether the brain operates on quantum principles is still live today. THE DISAPPEARANCE Grinberg was last seen on 8 December 1994, about to make a research trip to Nepal. But he never returned, and evidence soon confirmed that he never embarked on the trip at all. [Link] On December 12, his family had prepared a party to celebrate his 48th birthday, but he did not show up. It was common for him to make spontaneous travels or just not answer his phone for days, which is why his disappearance did not seem odd to his family in the beginning. [Link] His second wife, Teresa Mendoza, also disappeared on the same day. [Link] This is the detail that changes the whole texture of the case. Two people. Same day. Zero trace of either. The original police officer assigned to Grinberg's case was later removed, and he gave his take on why we still haven't gotten any real answers over 25 years later. [Link] A cop gets removed from a missing persons case involving a scientist whose lab subsequently gets cleaned out. That's not nothing. The project was terminated and declassified in 1995 [Link] — one year after Grinberg vanished. The timing is at minimum interesting. Suspicion first turned to his wife, María Teresa, and then, with more substance, to his frequent, clandestine trips to CIA-backed scientific research conferences around America. [Link] So in the final weeks: a major scientific paper on brain-to-brain nonlocal transfer gets published, he's giving talks at conferences with people who have documented government intelligence ties, and then he's gone. His wife is gone. His lab is cleaned out. The cop working the case gets pulled. THE THEORIES 1. He was removed. Somebody — intelligence agency, pharmaceutical interests, a government that didn't want this research to gain traction — made him disappear. His work, if it had scaled, had massive implications for surveillance, communication, and the pharmaceutical-neuroscience complex. He was demonstrating that consciousness might be nonlocal. That doesn't fit neatly into a world where mental health is managed through biochemical intervention. 2. His wife killed him or was complicit. Teresa Mendoza disappearing the same day either means she was involved or they left together. The documentary leans into this angle but finds no hard evidence. 3. He left voluntarily. This is the theory that should unsettle you most because it's the one that takes his work seriously. His Syntergic Theory described states of consciousness in which the ego dissolves and the individual merges with the Lattice. He had been studying shamanic traditions that described literal death and rebirth as consciousness transitions. He wrote extensively about "superperception" — states beyond ordinary sensory reality. Some of his colleagues and students believe he reached a point in his own research where he concluded there was somewhere else to go. Not metaphorically. It is almost as if he suffered two disappearances: a personal one in 1994, but also a disappearance of his memory. His books are hard to find in libraries. He is rarely mentioned in Mexican media. The first disappearance may or may not have been part of an international plot; but the second, which is far more outrageous, seems to have been the result of sweeping under the rug something uncomfortable we barely understand. [Link] WHAT HIS WORK MEANS NOW The transferred potential experiment has never been definitively replicated at scale, but it also has never been definitively debunked. The experiment's methodology — Faraday isolation, EEG synchronization after meditation, stimulus-response across subjects — has been partially reproduced by other researchers who found similar anomalies. The Penrose-Hameroff quantum consciousness model, the Holonomic Brain Theory, and global workspace theory are all still active frameworks in neuroscience and physics. The question of whether consciousness is a local phenomenon produced entirely by the brain, or something that interacts with a broader informational field, is unresolved. Grinberg didn't answer it. But he pointed at it from a lab, with EEG data, in a peer-reviewed journal, and then vanished four days before his 48th birthday. His final published sentence, if accurately reported, wasn't a mystical flourish. It reads like a man who believed he had proven something. Whether that something got him killed, freed him, or broke him is the question that doesn't have an answer. What's clear: the research exists. The citations exist. The CIA interest was real. The lab was cleared. And 31 years later, nobody has found a body, a motive, or a satisfying explanation.
